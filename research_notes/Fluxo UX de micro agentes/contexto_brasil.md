# Brazilian context for João: chat, phones, banking assistants and the first-home journey

Research date: 2026-11-04. Scope: low and middle income first-time buyers, mobile-first, Goiânia.

Evidence tags used on every finding:
- [EMPIRICAL] survey or statistical data with a stated method.
- [OFFICIAL] primary rule, contract, price list or regulated disclosure.
- [COMPANY] self-reported by the company, not audited.
- [VENDOR] blog or page from a company selling related services.
- [PRESS] journalism or secondary reporting.
- [SEO] low-quality explanatory content, directional only.
- (snippet) means only a search summary was read, not the full page. The match between a figure and its exact URL was inferred in these cases, so re-check the page before quoting publicly.

## WhatsApp as a channel

### Takeaway
WhatsApp is universal and already the expected way to talk to companies, and people are used to bots there, but it is a risky home for João. Meta's terms leave it to Meta's sole discretion whether an AI product is allowed, service messages have been paid beyond a free tier since 1 October 2026, and Zapia, an AI assistant whose largest market is Brazil, has moved off it. A site-based chat with WhatsApp as a return channel is defensible, but no Brazilian study tests that exact pattern, and WhatsApp's main advantage (often zero-rated on prepaid) means the site is the piece that fails first when data runs out.

### Inferences
- João's classification is the main platform risk. If it is presented as "a free AI that answers your questions," it resembles an AI product. If it is presented as the Primeira Chave education service, with AI as the mechanism, it fits the ancillary reading better. Meta decides, so WhatsApp should not be the only channel.
- Worst-case cost if Meta treats João as an AI provider in Brazil: every non-template message costs about R$0.32 rather than R$0.035, about 9 times more. Worked example: 1,000 users receiving 30 bot messages a month is 30,000 messages. As ordinary service: 29,000 paid × R$0.035 = about R$1,015. As AI provider: 30,000 × R$0.3217 = about R$9,650. This assumes the free tier does not apply to the AI fee, which needs confirming.
- Design recommendation: site is the home for the conversation and the saved progress, WhatsApp is an opt-in return channel carrying a few short nudges. This also avoids the data-training restriction applying to the core conversation data.
- A nudge such as "continue sua jornada" could be classified as marketing, at about 9 times the utility price. Nudges tied to something the user asked for ("você pediu para lembrar de separar o holerite") are more likely to pass as utility, but this is my reading, not a Meta ruling. Test each template.
- Because Caixa has told the public it does not send links by WhatsApp, a João message with a link may look like a scam. Return messages should identify the sender, avoid asking for data, and state plainly that João is not Caixa or the government.
- Saying "this is an automated assistant" costs little: about 9 in 10 users already know they meet bots on WhatsApp. What people dislike is robotic replies, so tone and a clear way out matter more than hiding the bot.

### Gaps
- No Brazilian study found on the site-chat-then-WhatsApp pattern, in real estate or elsewhere. An A/B test is the only way to answer this.
- Meta's Brazil-specific terms and official BRL rate card were not read directly; BRL figures come from a vendor quoting them. The current Mobile Time report tables on audio were not available, so the latest audio figure is 2025.
- No data found on whether people prefer sending audio or hearing audio back from companies, or on trust in WhatsApp as a channel for financial information among low-income users.
- Whether the AI fee applies only to named AI providers, and how Meta would classify a domain-specific education bot, is unresolved. Bot satisfaction scores exist in the 2024 Panorama (means of about 3.1 to 3.8 by channel) but the extracted chart could not be mapped to channels reliably; a vendor summary says WhatsApp scored lowest overall — [Infobip](https://www.infobip.com/pt/blog/panorama-mensageria-brasil-mobile-time-opinion-box).

## Brazilian bank and fintech assistants

### Takeaway
Big banks report high resolution rates, but these are self-declared and defined differently. The shared UX pattern is natural language, voice or text, narrow tasks, confirmation before acting and handoff to humans. Banks are also pulling their best AI inside their own apps, and Caixa, the institution that matters most here, launched an assistant inside its housing simulator that explains terms.

### Inferences
UX patterns worth copying, inferred from the products above:
- A narrow promise. BIA's Pix has a R$300 cap and Nubank limits automated turns before handoff. João should do the same: explain and guide, stop at clear limits.
- Voice is treated as a normal input, in BIA, Itaú and Nubank.
- Natural language instead of menus ("Pix para minha mãe"), with confirmation before anything important.
- Ca's job is the closest comparison. It is an official term explainer next to a simulator. João cannot beat it on authority, so it has to win on what Ca cannot do: neutrality across banks, MCMV and imóvel usado, before the person is a customer, plus a plain "what comes next."
- Bank metrics are not targets for João. A "resolved" bank ticket has a clear end state; an educational conversation does not, so João needs a metric of its own, such as whether the person completed the next real step.

### Gaps
- No independent or regulator validation of any bank's resolution rate was found. Satisfaction data for these assistants among low-income users was not found.
- Nothing public was found on Ca's usage, on its conversation design, or whether it uses generative AI.
- Febraban's PDF was not read in full; nothing on WhatsApp as a bank channel surfaced.
- No public detail on Luzia's Brazil audience, or current user count.

## Audience: literacy, phones and data limits

### Takeaway
Plan for a person with a phone and little else: most of class C and nearly all of DE use only a phone, half of DE ran out of data in the past three months, and only 35% of adults reach consolidated literacy. Text must be short and concrete, audio should be available in and out, and the page must be light and must keep working on weak connections.

### Inferences
These are design judgments, not measured thresholds.
- Text: Inaf's elementary level is the middle of the audience, and functional illiteracy reaches 51% at 50 to 64. Target one idea per bubble, a few short sentences, everyday words, and spell out numbers with a concrete example ("parcela de R$ 900 por mês"), not jargon such as CET or SAC unless explained.
- Long forms are a likely barrier. Inaf's digital test had form filling as its most autonomous track, and low scorers were concentrated among the functionally illiterate. Item-level results were not available, so this is a reasonable reading rather than a finding. Prefer buttons, one question at a time, and optional free text.
- Audio both ways: 80% already exchange audio. Accept voice input and offer a "listen" button on each answer. Audio is roughly 120 KB per minute at 16 kbit/s Opus (arithmetic, not a source), so generate it on request and do not autoplay.
- Page weight: 49% of DE ran out of data, and speed reductions affect 38% of DE, so assume slow connections for days at the end of a package. Keep the first load very small, avoid web fonts and video, load images and illustrations on demand, and keep the conversation readable offline. A budget of about 200 KB of first-load transferred weight is a sensible starting target to test, not a sourced standard.
- Save progress without an account. A phone user whose data runs out will vanish for days, so a return link or short code should restore the journey. WhatsApp is valuable here only if the user is on a plan where it survives the end of the package, which is not guaranteed.
- A person may not type well or may not be the phone's only user. Avoid forcing a login and avoid revealing financial details on a return link preview.
- With only 16% of DE using generative AI, João cannot assume people know what a chat assistant does. The first screen should show examples of questions.
- Roughly half of adults having a restriction means João should treat "nome sujo" as a normal topic, early and without judgment, pointing to renegotiation as a real step.

- No reliable data found on Android version, device age or RAM among low-income Brazilians; older Android implications are assumptions. No Brazil-specific page weight study found.

### Inferences
- João's best moment is before the bank. Four of the five top obstacles (debts, entrada, price, income) can be worked on months ahead, without documents or a credit decision. This fits the no-collection, no-approval model.
- "Giving up" is common (31%) and tied to installments higher than expected, so honest estimates early are worth more than optimistic ones. Show ranges and label them as estimates.
- The clearest gap in the Caixa stack is translation of waiting. People can see a stage in the app, but not what it means or whether a delay is normal. João can explain stages and typical ranges, without knowing the person's case. Strikes show that any stated time must be a range with a date.
- Time figures are unreliable, so João should say "costuma variar" and name the things that move it, not a number of days. The only robust statement is that it can take weeks to months.
- Because rules changed in March and April 2026 and sources disagree, all MCMV numbers in João need one owned, dated source table, checked against Ministério das Cidades and Caixa. They should never come from the LLM's memory.
- Warnings João can give usefully: nobody can sell approval, MCMV units should not carry unexplained fees, and check who is charging what. These fit the complaint pattern, but the wording needs legal review.
- Since people decide as a household, João should produce something shareable: a short summary to show a partner or family, in plain language, with no personal data.
